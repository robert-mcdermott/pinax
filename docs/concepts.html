How Pinax sees storage

Scans, snapshots, folder ownership, who sees what, sizes, ages, change, costs, plans, tags, and the catalog: the ideas every other page relies on.

The ideas the rest of the documentation relies on, for the people who use Pinax and the people who run it. Each is explained in depth elsewhere; this is the map.

Filesystems and scans

A filesystem is a body of storage Pinax reports on, such as Fast at /fh/fast: registered once by an administrator, by the root its scans record, with a name people know it by.

Pinax never crawls storage itself. fs-inventory, a separate scanner built for very large filesystems, walks the filesystem's metadata (names, sizes, owners, and times, never file contents) and writes a scan: a folder of Parquet files and a manifest. Scans run on a schedule, typically weekly, from a host that can read the filesystem. A scan of two billion files takes hours, and costs about 40 bytes per entry on disk.

Pinax is read-only. It reads scans; it never opens, moves, changes, or deletes a file on the storage it reports on. Cleanup plans and tags are records in Pinax that people act on themselves.

Snapshots and indexes

Ingest turns a scan into a snapshot: an index (DuckDB and Parquet files under PINAX_VAR_ROOT) in which every directory already carries the totals of everything below it. That is why a treemap of a petabyte draws in tens of milliseconds: a page never adds up files, it reads totals computed once per scan. Ingest also records each top-level folder's totals, which are kept for good (the folder history), and what changed since the previous scan.

Pinax keeps the newest six indexes of each filesystem (PINAX_KEEP_SNAPSHOTS); older snapshots are pruned: their index is deleted, their folder history kept. Every report can show the latest scan or any earlier one still kept in full.

Totals can be checked: pinax check compares every directory of an index with fs-inventory rollup, an independent computation, and reports any difference. When fs-inventory could not read a folder, its totals are lower bounds, and Pinax says so where they appear.

Labs, departments, and folder ownership

The unit of ownership is a top-level folder of a filesystem, such as /fh/fast/fujimoto_a. Each is owned by one lab (a PI's group) or department (a core facility, a division), or by no one. A lab may own folders on several filesystems.

People belong to labs as a PI (or a department's owner) or a delegate (a lab manager, a staff scientist). Members see exactly the top-level folders their labs own, everything below them, and nothing else: the check is made on the server for every request, and other labs' folders do not appear at all. Storage administrators see every filesystem and folder, and the administration pages, and change the site's settings.

Beside their labs, a person can have site roles: an auditor sees everything a storage administrator does and changes nothing; finance sees every lab's charges and the prices, free tiers, and capacities behind them, and no folders; a division head sees the labs of their division as those labs' members do, to read. Seeing and changing are separate: a lab's tags and cleanup plan are changed only by its PI and delegates (and administrators).

Scripts use Pinax with an API token instead of signing in: a person's own, which carries their access or only reads, or a service token an administrator makes for a named service, which reads as finance or as an auditor. A token expires, can be revoked, and is never more than its person.

Ownership changes over time (a folder moves to another lab, a lab closes), and Pinax keeps when each change happened, so a past month is charged as it was.

Sizes

Two sizes, shown side by side where they differ:

  • Size (logical): the sum of the files' sizes, what people think of as how much data they have.
  • Space on disk (allocated): what the storage actually uses, smaller for compressed or sparse files, larger for many small files on large blocks.

A file with several hard links is counted once, at its first link by path; its other links are files with no size. Sizes are shown in binary units (TiB, GiB) unless a price or capacity was set in decimal ones (TB).

Ages and cold data

Each file has times: when it last changed (modified), and when it was last read (accessed, where the filesystem records it reliably). Ages are measured from the scan's start, by modification time unless a view says otherwise, in seven age buckets: under 90 days, 90 days to 1 year, 1 to 2, 2 to 3, 3 to 5, 5 to 10, and 10 years or more.

A folder's average age is weighted by size, so one large old file outweighs many small new ones. Cold data is data unchanged for PINAX_COLD_DAYS (two years by default); a cold folder is the largest folder in which everything is that old, a unit that could be archived or deleted whole.

The age map's colors run from red (changed recently) to blue (cold), split at two years.

Change over time

Every report compares scans: the change map colors folders by how much they grew or shrank since an earlier scan; what changed lists the folders and files new and removed between two scans; history draws each folder over all its scans. A forecast carries the trend of the last six months of scans a year ahead, with a range and a check of how well the same method did three months ago; a step (a project moved in or out) restarts the trend.

Costs and charges

Administrators set each filesystem's price (per TB or TiB a month, from a day on) and free tiers (an amount free for every lab, for every department, or for one lab as an exception). A lab's monthly charge is worked out from its folders' sizes over the month, scan by scan, less what is free, at the price on each day: a month with a price change is charged in parts. This month's charge is an estimate to the month's end. Chargeback is every lab's charges for a month, with a CSV statement for finance.

Cleanup plans and tags

A cleanup plan lists folders a lab means to delete, move to cheaper storage, or compress, and shows what each would free and how much the lab's charge would fall. Later scans show what was done. Pinax suggests folders worth adding, and follows a folder that was renamed rather than removed (by its inode).

Tags describe folders in terms a lab and IT share: project, grant, data stage, source, classification, IRB protocol, a public copy, keep until, legal hold. Administrators keep the standard list; labs add their own. A tag applies to everything below its folder. Collections finds data by tag; charges split by grant; folders on legal hold or kept until a date stay out of cleanup plans.

The catalog

Everything that is not an index (people and their roles, labs, folder ownership, filesystems, snapshot records, folder history, prices, plans, tags, views, API tokens, the audit log) is in the catalog: SQLite in PINAX_VAR_ROOT by default, or PostgreSQL. It is small, and it is the one part that cannot be made again from scans, so it is backed up. Indexes can be rebuilt from their scans.

Every change made in the app, by import, or from the command line is audited: who, what, when, from where, and with which API token. Administrators and auditors read it in the app's Audit log, and review who has access to what, with when each was given, in the Access review.
